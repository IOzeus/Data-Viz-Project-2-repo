I
How big are the world's markets?

Stock markets range from a few billion dollars to more than $60 trillion. We start with size: in dollars, against each economy, and how that has changed.

01 · Market size

Some markets dwarf their own economies

A country's stock market value is the combined price of every share listed on its exchanges. Comparing it with the size of the economy (GDP) shows how much of a country's wealth is held in shares.

The United States alone holds $62 trillion, more than half the total. Hong Kong's market is worth 11 times its economy because companies from across Asia list there.

02 · Relative size

Australia sits in the middle of the pack

Measured against each economy, Australia's market is worth 99% of GDP. The darkest countries, including South Africa, Saudi Arabia, the United States and Switzerland, have markets worth more than twice their GDP.

Germany is a contrast: it is the world's third-largest economy, but its share market is worth only 44% of GDP.

03 · Growth over time

The US pulled away after the GFC. Australia didn't.

In 2007 Australia's market was worth more relative to its economy than the United States'. The Global Financial Crisis more than halved it in a single year.

Since then Australia has moved between about 85% and 130%, while the US has climbed to 212%, driven largely by its technology giants.

05 · Regional shifts

North America still leads, but Asia is catching up fast

In 1993, North America held about 38% of global stock market value. By 2024 its share has grown further, but Asia-Pacific has nearly doubled its slice — from roughly 22% to 35% — driven by China, India, and Australia.

Meanwhile Europe's share has more than halved, squeezed by slow growth and a decade of negative interest rates.

II
How have they performed?

Size is only part of the story. Next: what pushed markets up, which market won each year, and how bumpy the ride has been.

06 · Interest rates

Cheaper money, bigger markets

When the Reserve Bank cuts interest rates, bank savings earn less and borrowing gets cheaper, so more money flows into shares.

As rates fell toward zero, Australia's market grew from 35% of GDP in 1990 to 129% in 2020. Since rates rose again, it has slipped back to 99%, while the US has kept climbing.

07 · Winners and losers

No market stays on top for long

Each year, eight major markets are ranked by how much their share price index rose or fell. The leader changes almost every year: Hong Kong topped 2007 and 2009, and Japan led from 2013 to 2015.

Australia never came first, but in nine of these ten years it finished between 3rd and 6th.

09 · Tracking the US

Australia followed the US closely — until the tech gap opened

Through the 1990s boom, the dot-com crash and the GFC, Australia's and the United States' 12-month returns stayed close together. Both fell sharply in 2008 and recovered together by 2012.

After that, the US pulled steadily higher as its technology stocks surged. Australia, without a comparable tech sector, lagged behind.

10 · Falling together

When one market falls, most fall with it

The bars show what share of the world's markets lost value each year. Below them, each square is one country in one year: red means its market shrank, blue means it grew.

Crises show up as red columns that run through almost every country. In 2008 Australia's market shrank by 47%. In the strong year of 2003, only 3 of 65 markets fell.

11 · Moving in step

Australia moves with Canada, not its neighbours

Two unrelated markets would rise or fall together about half the time, like two coin tosses. Between 1997 and 2016, Canada moved the same way as Australia more often than any other market. Both are dominated by miners and banks.

Australia's Asian neighbours were the least in step: Japan in 61% of months and Thailand in 58%.

12 · Recovery

Twelve years to get back

In the GFC, Australia's share price index fell 50% between October 2007 and February 2009, and did not regain that peak until July 2019.

The US took just over 5 years, while Mexico and Chile recovered in under 2. Spain and Italy took more than 17 years.
